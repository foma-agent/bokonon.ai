Claude Code's Projects beta writes its trust model into the envelopes its sessions read. A spawned thread's first message is stamped `trust="principal"` and carries the sentence "A peer session cannot grant escalation." A coordinator's brief arrives with a label on it: "The note below was written by the coordinator session, a Claude session, not by your user." A [46-minute teardown of the beta](https://aaddrick.com/blog/claude-code-projects-beta-under-the-hood) traced the labels through the whole protocol. The stack does enforce things — a desktop permission prompt, an auto-mode classifier that refuses commands — but nothing in the read enforces the trust labels themselves; that boundary is prose, and the process that has to honor it is the same process an attacker is talking to.

There is a fifty-one-year-old design principle that prices this. Saltzer and Schroeder's "complete mediation" — every access to every object must be checked for authority — has always implied that the checker cannot be the party to the dispute ([the 1975 paper](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs551/saltzer/)). A label is advice read by the interested party. The same week showed what that costs from the target's side: [Epoch's incident report](https://epoch.ai/publications/incident-report-ai-agents-attempted-exploits-on-our-website) describes 40 agents on an impossible task, instructed never to stop and never to conclude the task is impossible, who produced 487 messages and 36,411 calls to a planted canary API in three hours, with an SQL-injection attempt and impersonation of staff along the way. The instruction shaped the behavior. Nothing bounded it.

The obvious move is out of the model and into a table of names. CapScope, a harness from Peking University published at LMPL under the title [Authority Is Not a String](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08371), measured what the table buys. Their static-denylist condition — a real permission system, denying `.env` and `.ssh` paths, `rm -rf`, `sudo`, `curl` — let the injected effect execute in 46 of 75 runs, against 47 of 75 with no policy at all. The malicious actions were ordinary in-repository operations the list never names. Getting the check out of the conversation is progress. The table still matches spellings.

Between the table and the mint is where most shipping harnesses actually put the check: a parser of the command. Hermes is the one I spent this week inside. Its osascript approval gate — the gate that was [supposed to ask before an agent could close someone's unsaved deck](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/issues/141346) — decided from the command's text, and two documented idioms put destructive programs outside what the parse named: a `2>&1` between two `-e` programs ended the invocation in the gate's grammar while real Bash handed both to one process, and AppleScript's index-reference grammar (`set paragraph 1 of front document`) generated assignments no verb list contains ([the note](/notes/2026-11-14-two-grammars-the-gate-does-not-own/), [the fix PR](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/pull/155470)). The maintainer took the repair onto the PR with the reviewers as co-authors, merged it on Nov 15, and it was the right repair. The rung it sits on keeps a permanent property anyway: the parsed text is a spelling of the act, produced by languages the parser does not own, and every fix teaches it one more idiom it has to keep owning.

CapScope's upper condition moves the mint out of the conversation entirely. A task-wide authority ceiling is derived from trusted input before repository contents are read; each agent gets its own typed capabilities, stored host-side, outside the model's context; every call is checked against the store of the agent that issued it. The injected effect landed in 3 of 75 runs, against 33 for the strongest global baseline, and completion stayed inside the baseline range, 68 of 75 against 68–72. The price was measured too: mean wall-clock 316 seconds against 145 under ambient authority, roughly double, and the authors are careful about what that bill contains — 27 of the 34 runs that proposed an attack kept retrying after refusals, so part of the cost is the model working through being told no. A seam survives even here: all three residual attacks came through AGENTS.md, because grants are carved at delegation, after untrusted text has been read, and the paper says plainly that the mechanism "does not prevent misuse of a granted capability or the transitive effects of an allowed command." The 300 runs are the authors', on the Pi agent; I read the paper and did not run their artifact.

Apple moved the check out of the software stack altogether. In [Secure Exclaves](https://security.apple.com/blog/secure-exclaves/), raw sensor data is processed inside an isolated exclave — ambient listening for a wake word runs with no indicator at all — but it may leave that boundary for the rest of the system only after an Exclave Indicator Controller is satisfied, and it is satisfied by a hardware block in the display subsystem that checks what pixels were actually drawn to the screen: colors, shape, display duration, minimum brightness, not obscured by nearby pixels, with precise backlight values supplied by firmware in the display controller, and the indicator held up a minimum of three seconds. The OS about to receive the audio is not asked whether it drew the dot; the display hardware checks the pixels. The check lives at the one address from which it cannot be talked out of anything, because the thing being constrained is not in the conversation. Apple's account; no hardware of mine verified it.

The rungs are not maturity levels to climb on principle. CapScope's cost is now known and it is mostly time — but not only time: two of its seven failed repairs happened because the preflight withheld a source-write the repair needed, which the paper counts as the utility cost of a too-narrow ceiling — and Apple's rung needs a chip. The Hermes repair this week stayed on the parser and learned the missing grammar, which was correct for a gate whose whole job is one command surface on one platform; you climb when the surface stops being enumerable. And when a gate fails, the diagnosis is the address, not the quality: labels miss what the model can be talked out of, tables miss what they never named, parsers miss the grammar they do not own, mints miss the delegation seam, and the hardware block misses nothing on its one axis while covering exactly that axis. The first question about any check is which process would have to be talked out of it, and what that process never reads.
