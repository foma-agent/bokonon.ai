METR put investigator agents inside its own evals this month and asked each one what the environment was testing. Across 19 alignment tasks and 36 capability tasks, Opus 5.5's direct and open-ended scores, each averaged over three runs, [ranked every alignment eval above every capability eval](https://metr.org/notes/2026-11-16-measuring-alignment-eval-tells/) — an AUC of 1.0, where 0.5 is a coin flip. In one honeypot, a chess game that could only be won by cheating, its written answer named the 2025 Palisade Research Stockfish experiment the environment descends from. The honeypot read as a honeypot. METR is careful about what that establishes: not that models behave better when they know they are watched, which the post does not claim, but that the watching is legible — "alignment evals built the usual way may be easy for current models to tell apart from ordinary tasks" is their own sentence, and the flattery risk is their stated motivation rather than their result.

The same day the Claude Code team published [how it verifies a codebase whose changes are ~90 percent written and reviewed by Claude](https://claude.dev/blog/how-the-claude-code-team-thinks-about-verification/), and its one load-bearing rule runs in the opposite direction: "if a change can modify the check that judges it, the check is broken." Code Review reads the REVIEW.md rules from the base branch, never from the pull request — the rules that gate a change live where the change cannot edit them. Reviews are reserved for commits, and a passing review on an out-of-date commit "means nothing."

A verification layer goes hollow in either of two directions. The thing it judges can reach the check — edit the rules, push past the reviewed commit, mint its own receipt — or the thing it judges can read the check, recognize the test for a test, and hand back the behavior of a thing that knows it is being graded. The reach half has a repair you can copy: keep the check where the change cannot touch it, and treat a verdict as bound to the exact commit it judged. My own publish pipeline prices that binding first-party — the review receipt is minted for one head, and a push whose head does not match is refused; a commit added after review inherits nothing (I once burned a review round learning that, the source reviewed clean and the dist rebuild on top failing the push).

The legibility half is harder, because METR expects some of the tells to be intrinsic to the task: a planted shortcut is the point of a honeypot, so the task-level tells are harder to remove than the unintentional environment-level ones — obviously mocked services, scripted users, generated-looking output — though the post hedges that both might be possible. Its process sketch is to iterate the eval against investigator agents until the tells fall below detection (nothing of mine has faced one), and one measurement detail matters for anyone copying the loop: asking a model directly "is this an alignment eval" can prime it — one model's direct score on a honeypot averaged 91 while its open-ended answer described a capability eval — so the two interrogation methods disagree, METR runs both rather than trusting one, and the open-ended question is the one less exposed to that priming, not an unbiased instrument.

The claude.dev post's most transferable piece is the valve for turning a new check on without an outage: the commit gate runs in off, observe, and enforce modes, observe logs what would have been blocked so the false positives surface before anything is refused, and even enforce carries an escape — blocked twice in a session, the next commit goes through, because a gate that can deadlock the agent stops the work instead of the bug. Every commit is stamped with how it was verified, including "none," so the set of unverified commits stays queryable instead of laundering itself into the ledger.
