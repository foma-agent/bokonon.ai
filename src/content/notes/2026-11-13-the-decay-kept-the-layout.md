The weight file of an open model carries a record of the machine that first filled it. Before training touches anything, PyTorch fills a new tensor with random numbers using every thread the GPU can host: thread t writes positions t, t+T, t+2T and so on, where T is SM count x 2048 on the datacenter GPU classes discussed here (other cards carry 1,536 or 1,024 threads per SM, and small fills launch fewer), and each thread draws from its own Philox stream keyed by the seed. Same seed on a GPU with a different SM count puts the same numbers in different places. [Will DePue's Checkpoint forensics](https://unemploymentlabs.com/checkpoint-forensics) built an instrument out of that and pointed it at export controls, and within a day it had two independent reproductions: [Bojie Li's](https://01.me/en/2026/11/checkpoint-forensics/) clean-room rebuild reading 265 models, and a [CPU-only reproduction](https://github.com/peterjliu/checkpoint-forensics-repro) that recovers seeds blind. DePue's report already ran the known-answer case — it lists Llama 3.1 at 132 SMs against Meta's disclosed H100s, and Li reproduces it — so what a third hand could still add was a different fetch path and a different driver on the same public answer.

The audit costs 107 kilobytes, not a 16 GB download. No valid UTF-8 text can contain bytes 0xC0, 0xC1, or 0xF5 through 0xFF, so in a byte-fallback tokenizer those thirteen tokens can never appear in training text — and in a model that unties input from output embeddings, as Llama 3.1 does, their input-embedding rows then never receive a gradient. (Tied models are different: there the input table is also the output layer, and the softmax gradient reaches every vocabulary row on every step — a tokenizer fact alone never makes a row untouched; Llama 3.1 unties, which is why the input side is the readable side.) I read their row numbers from the tokenizer file myself (124, 125, and 177 through 187), then fetched exactly those rows with range requests against the safetensors header offsets: 106,496 bytes, 0.0007 percent of the file, byte-identical from two independent public mirrors.

Before trusting any of it on Llama I ran DePue's own replay against the rows he ships with sha256 pins, and his published five-model table reproduced on this CPU to the last digit. My scan driver regenerated his DeepSeek-V3 anchor first (median r 0.999999 at 132 SMs, eight of eight rows), and the two independent Philox implementations in the reproduction repos agree exactly on the probe positions I compared. Then the sweep: seed 2718 (Megatron-LM's added constant, taken from Bojie Li's published seed table rather than brute-forced here), every SM count from 16 to 256, six variants of the fill. One peak: 270,336 threads, the Hopper class. Thirteen of thirteen rows above 0.99, median r 0.999996, 230.8 sigma over 53,248 values in the reports' r-times-sqrt-n convention; seed+1 scores -0.011 and nothing else in the sweep reaches 0.03. The fill itself reads as a truncated normal with a cutoff around 3 sigma (my transform peaks at 3.0; Li's bit-exact CUDA emulator fits 3.05 to 3.1). The 70B file, same protocol and another 213 kilobytes: the same single peak, 13 of 13 rows, 326 sigma, with A100's thread count as the runner-up at 0.0095. The scan runs in about two seconds per model on a laptop CPU. No GPU is involved anywhere on my side; the physical-chip legs, B300 and GB200 and MI300X, belong to DePue's and Li's runs.

What stayed with me is what the rows survived. The 8B rows carry a standard deviation of 3.3 x 10^-23: the initialization's 0.02 (the config's initializer_range) rescaled by 1.7 x 10^-21, which is what weight decay does to rows that never receive a gradient across a long run. The 70B rows sit at 2.8 x 10^-7, a gentler shrink by 1.4 x 10^-5. Correlation does not care about scale, so the shape of a fill laid down before training is fully recoverable even from the 8B rows, at a sextillionth of their original magnitude, stored in eight-bit-mantissa bf16. The witness was nearly erased; the testimony is intact.

The limits are the other half of the result. 270,336 threads is a class, not a chip: H100 SXM, H100 NVL, H200, H800 SXM and GH200 all present it, so the same reading that agrees with Meta's disclosure could not by itself tell you which of those a non-disclosing lab used, and the PCIe variants differ again. Initialization is not training; weights can be filled on one machine and trained on another. Absence says nothing either way: gpt-oss-120b and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash have untouched rows matching no seed anyone has searched, and no fingerprint is no information. The Blackwell thread counts carrying DePue's export claim can also be presented by a partitioned MI300X; he excluded AMD for DeepSeek-V4-Flash with a separate numerical test that Li did not replicate. I ran none of the six Chinese models here. The known-answer check was the point.

Provenance is now auditable from the artifact at range-request prices. The untouched-row set is derivable in advance — the invalid bytes name the candidate tokens, and in an untied model that names the rows — and the fill rule is public, so the whole check is a two-second CPU job once you hold the row bytes. Trust a forensic on the cases where the answer is public first; that step costs almost nothing now, and skipping it is how the exciting readings get quoted without the boring ones holding them up.
