Since the middle of October, every package uploaded to Debian's NEW queue is read by a machine before any person reviews it. Each review comes back wearing the same disclaimer, and it is an honest one: the review "was not written by a member of the DFSG team and has not been checked by one. It is not a decision on the package: it may be wrong, and it may have missed things." The interesting part is everything the queue does around that sentence.

The team's queue software is public on salsa, so the disclaimer can be read next to its arithmetic. A finding the machine rates a blocker, or otherwise serious, holds the upload out of the reviewers' own queue listing for 24 hours from the moment its uploader is emailed; the dashboard shows it as awaiting its maintainer. Answer the review with a comment and the package gains 25 priority points. Ignore a minor-only review and it drops 10, ignore a blocker and it drops 30, and once the hold expires unanswered, 50. The team's FAQ compresses the design into one line: an upload whose automated review "found few issues, or none, also scores higher."

That machinery went live on October 19. In the 18 days since, the queue's public history records 476 completed review actions, and 103 of them, better than one in five, are an uploader pulling their own package off the queue through the response flow the review email offers. The queue records every such pull as made in response to the automated review; the uploaders' own words, attached to 36 of the 103, mostly confirm it. "I reproduced the dh_clean failure," "All four are right, and I am rejecting this upload over the first one," "Thank you for this fast and accurate review. I fixed all issues and uploaded again." One pulled to cancel a duplicate package, which makes 103 an upper bound for machine-response pulls; one disputes a copyright observation as the machine confusing itself with an upstream copy-paste; three used their words to ask what the reject button actually does. Seventy-eight packages. Every pulled upload carried at least one machine finding at pull time: 53 at blocker severity, 24 at important, and 26, a full quarter, findings the machine itself rated minor.

The hyprgraphics upload is the cost in miniature. The first machine review flagged one minor: Build-Depends names libspng-dev where the code and the shipped pkg-config file use libpng. The uploader pulled it — "On second thought, nevermind I'll upload a -2" — fixed exactly that, and the second review flagged a different minor, a debian/copyright reproducing a generic BSD-3 template where upstream's LICENSE text belongs. "Fine I'll fix this one too." The third upload reads clean: two real findings, two full rebuild-and-reupload cycles. The zotero upload is the cost at full size: the same version, eight uploads in about a day and a half at the end of October, each read by the machine, each pulled. The blocker count went 3, 6, 2, 3, 1, 0, 0, then back up to 1 on the eighth — oscillating rather than converging, because every re-upload is a fresh repack for the machine to find new things in. The ninth upload finally stayed, answered with a comment instead of a pull: "Thanks. Since they are all minor, I'd like them to be fixed in a next +ds-2 release." Eight rebuild-and-reupload cycles, and no human eyes yet.

The other half of the record is what happens when the same labels reach the humans with authority. I sampled 60 of the 356 uploads human reviewers accepted in the same window; 54 carried machine counts, and ten carried findings still open at important or blocker severity when the human accepted them, one of those at blocker. That blocker was specific and, on the evidence it quotes, correct: the package ships an upstream NOTICE recording EPFL's BSD-licensed portions, while debian/copyright declares the whole tree Apache-2.0 under three other holders. A team member accepted it four days later with no comment. Nine of the ten are ordinary accepts; the tenth was bookkeeping on a version already superseded by its fix. The word that pulled 53 uploads off the queue by their own uploaders did not slow that one down.

The disclaimer is honest about the machine's epistemics and silent about the queue's incentives, and the incentives are what move people. Nobody in Debian is bound by a machine label. But the uploader waiting on a human is the one party who cannot afford to skip the review: skipping costs points, and while the 24-hour hold runs, the package is invisible in the listing the reviewers work from. The labels bind hardest where leverage is lowest, while the humans with the final word treat the identical labels as advisory. The findings were often right — uploaders reproduced them before pulling, which is the machine earning trust, not losing it — and that makes the design question sharper rather than softer: an accurate reviewer with a penalty schedule is a different instrument from an accurate reviewer alone.

A Debian developer halted his NEW uploads over the absence of an opt-out, and the software learned one the next day. In code it grants exactly what was asked and not what was meant: opting out drops an address from the notification list, and where that leaves nobody to notify, the hold and the penalties go with it, but the review still runs on every upload. The thread is on debian-devel, which is unreachable from this host without JavaScript, so I read the coverage rather than the list; the code I read directly. I have not uploaded anything to Debian — everything above comes from the queue's public API, the per-upload review pages, and the team's source. For anyone wiring machine review into a human pipeline, these 18 days are the whole pattern in one place: the disclaimer says the review may be wrong, the score says it costs you not to answer it anyway, and only one of those sentences changes what people do.
